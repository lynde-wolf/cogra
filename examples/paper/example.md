# Introduction

It has long been remarked that an interval filled with events seems to last
longer than an empty one of the same physical length. The observation is
easily made and as easily forgotten, for it is seldom large and it varies a
good deal from one listener to the next. Two kinds of account have been
offered for it. On the first, an internal counter accumulates pulses during
the interval, and anything that occurs within the interval adds pulses of
its own. On the second, events within the interval draw attention to
themselves and away from the passage of time, so that fewer moments of the
interval are registered as time.[^1]

The two accounts make the same prediction for the simple comparison of
filled and empty intervals, and for this reason the comparison alone cannot
decide between them. They differ, however, in what they say about a second
variable that is known to lengthen judged duration, the pitch of a tone. If
pitch acts by raising the rate at which the counter runs, and clicks act by
adding pulses to it, the two effects should simply add. If clicks act by
diverting attention, their effect should be smaller for the higher tone,
which is itself the more arresting of the two. The present experiment was
designed to test this difference.

# Method

## Subjects

Twenty-four undergraduates, 13 women and 11 men, served in a single session
of about 50 min as part of a course requirement. None reported any
difficulty of hearing, and none had served in an experiment on time
judgment before.

## Apparatus

Tones were generated by an audio oscillator and gated by an electronic
timer accurate to 1 msec, with rise and fall times of 10 msec. Clicks were
0.5-msec rectangular pulses from a second generator, mixed with the tone
and delivered binaurally through matched earphones at a comfortable
listening level. The subject sat alone in a sound-attenuated booth and
responded on a telegraph key.

### Procedure

On each trial a warning light was followed after 1 sec by the standard,
a tone of 1,000 msec. After a pause of 2 sec the subject reproduced its
duration by holding down the key for as long as the tone had seemed to
last. No knowledge of results was given. The standard was presented in
each of six conditions, formed by crossing the pitch of the tone with the
number of clicks heard during it:

- a tone of 500 Hz or of 2,000 Hz;
- with no clicks, with 4 clicks, or with 8 clicks, spaced at random within
  the tone but not within 50 msec of its onset or offset.

Each subject received 20 trials in each condition, in an order randomized
anew for each subject, after 12 practice trials that were not scored.

# Results

The mean reproduced duration in each condition is shown in Figure 1, and
the values are given in Table 1. Reproductions were longer than the
standard in every condition, as is usual with this method, and they
lengthened with the number of clicks. An ANOVA with pitch and number of
clicks as within-subject factors showed a reliable effect of clicks,
*F*(2, 46) = 14.62, *p* < .001, and of pitch, *F*(1, 23) = 6.08,
*p* < .05. The interaction did not approach significance, *F* < 1.[^2]

The effect of the clicks was thus nearly the same for the two tones: 106
msec for the lower and 109 msec for the higher, from no clicks to eight.
Individual subjects showed the same pattern. Of the 24, 19 reproduced the
tone with eight clicks as longer than the tone with none at both pitches,
and the remaining 5 did so at one pitch only. No subject reproduced the
filled tone as shorter than the empty one at both pitches.

# Discussion

Both pitch and the number of clicks lengthened the reproduced duration of
the tone, and they did so independently of one another. This is the result
to be expected if pitch sets the rate of a counter and each click adds to
its total. It is not easily reconciled with the view that the clicks draw
attention from time, since the higher tone, which on that view should have
left less attention to be drawn, was affected by the clicks just as much as
the lower.

It would be premature to conclude that attention plays no part in such
judgments. The intervals used here were short, the clicks were few, and the
subjects had nothing to do during the tone but listen to it. Under heavier
demands, or over longer intervals, a diversion of attention might well be
found. The present results suggest only that, for brief tones heard without
distraction, the lengthening produced by interpolated events is better
described as an addition than as a loss.
