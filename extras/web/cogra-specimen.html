The Judged Heaviness of Lifted Cylinders of Two Sizes*

Ines M. Vargas and Walter J. Teller

Department of Psychology, Halvorsen College

Observers lifted cylinders of six weights in two sizes and judged the heaviness of each on a scale of 0 to 100. Judgments rose with weight for both sizes, but at every weight the smaller cylinder was judged the heavier, by a nearly constant amount. The constancy of the difference is taken to mean that size enters the judgment as an expectation fixed before the lift begins.

That an object of a given weight feels heavier when it is small than when it is large is among the oldest of the observations on lifted weights, and among the least explained. Brandt (1931) held that the observer, expecting the larger object to be the heavier, applies more force to it and is surprised by its lightness; Marchetti (1948) held instead that size and weight are combined in the sensation itself, with no part played by expectation. The two views differ in what they predict when weight is varied: on the first, the difference between sizes should be the same at every weight, since the expectation is formed before the lift; on the second, it should grow with weight.†

Method

Subjects

The observers were 16 graduate students, none of whom had served in an experiment on lifted weights or knew its purpose.

Apparatus and procedure. The cylinders were of turned brass, loaded with lead shot and sealed, in two sizes: 4 cm and 8 cm in diameter, of equal height. Each size was made in six weights from 100 to 350 gm in steps of 50 gm. The observer, seated with eyes open, lifted each cylinder by a handle to a height of about 5 cm and set it down, and then gave a number from 0 to 100 for its heaviness. Each cylinder was lifted four times, in a random order, over two sessions.

The mean judgment for each cylinder is shown in Fig. 1, and the mean difference between sizes is given in Table 1. Judgments rose nearly linearly with weight for both sizes. The small cylinder was judged the heavier at every weight, and the difference between sizes changed little from the lightest weight to the heaviest.

Discussion

The difference between sizes was nearly constant over a more than threefold range of weight, which is what the account in terms of expectation leads one to look for and what the account in terms of a combined sensation does not. It remains possible that the difference would grow over a wider range, and the heavier weights of the original series of Halloran (1952) are being reexamined with this in mind.
